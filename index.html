Morning Pages

A daily 30-minute exercise from Dorothea Brande / Julia Cameron, adapted by Dikkers for humor writing. You write without stopping for half an hour. No correcting typos. No judging. If you have nothing to write, write "I don't know what to write" until something else surfaces. Gently nudge yourself toward amusing thoughts, but if dark or strange thoughts come instead, write those — that's information about you. The point is to drain the gunk so the good stuff flows. Most of what you write will be garbage. Love it anyway.

Ten Cliche-Free Jokes

Stage 1 — Write 10 Jokes

Write 10 one-sentence jokes. The only rule: no cliches.

A cliche in humor writing is a joke, topic, or pattern you've heard before. Examples to avoid: airline food, mothers-in-law, "three somethings walk into a bar," "that's what she said," "I could tell you but then I'd have to kill you," "I just threw up in my mouth a little bit," game-show buzzer sounds for wrong answers, the surfer-dude character, white-people-vs-black-people contrasts, "Best. Thing. Ever.", "Welp," "I know, right?", "the Internets," LOL/LMFAO/IMHO as humor, "It's an oxymoron, like Military Intelligence."

Only fresh, original observations or unique angles can surprise. Surprise is the one ingredient all humor requires. Without surprise, no laugh.

Stage 2 — Identify the Subtext

Subtext is the value judgment or opinion the joke is secretly delivering. It is not the literal text. It is what the reader subconsciously decodes that makes them laugh.

State each Subtext as a simple subject-verb-object sentence. It must be irreducible — it cannot itself be a joke or have hidden meaning. It must be a flat, "on the nose" opinion.

Examples:

Joke: "Mother Teresa Sent To Hell In Wacky Afterlife Mix-Up" — Subtext: Religion's promises about the afterlife are arbitrary.

Joke: "Alcoholic Father Disappointed In Pothead Son" — Subtext: Hypocrisy is the default mode of parenting.

Joke: "Don't forget — tomorrow is Bring Your Daughter To Tears Day." — Subtext: Take Your Daughter to Work Day exists because girls still need extra workplace exposure, which is sad.

If you can't state the Subtext, the joke isn't saying anything yet. Mark it for revision.

Stage 3 — Write 10 Standalone Subtext Ideas

Now write 10 raw Subtext ideas. These are not jokes. They are flat opinions you actually hold about people, the world, or how things work.

Examples: "We're destroying our planet." / "People without a legitimate handicap should not be allowed to ride mobility scooters in the grocery store." / "Most adult friendships are logistical, not emotional." / "Power corrupts." / "People who have kids are selfish."

These don't need to be funny. The best Subtext often isn't — it's often sad or uncomfortable. "Comedy equals tragedy plus time" (Steve Allen). The Onion's longtime head writer Todd Hanson called humor "life's nightmare hellscape of unrelenting horror." Don't shy away from dark.

Tip #9: Comfort the afflicted, afflict the comfortable. Your Subtext should target power, the status quo, or universal human flaws — not the downtrodden.
